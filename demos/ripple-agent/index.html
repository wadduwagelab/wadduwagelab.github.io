A language-model annotator for RIPPLE

Can a language model replace the person who corrects point tracks? In RIPPLE a person clicks a target once, watches the track, and clicks again wherever it has drifted. Here a language model makes those corrections: it views zoomed crops of a suspect frame, picks the target among candidate positions, and hands the frame to a person when it cannot tell. We tested two Claude models, Opus and the much smaller Haiku, on the five videos of the RIPPLE paper, and scored them against tracks that an annotator made by hand, without RIPPLE.

  • 92.0average point precision of Claude Opus on the Neural Activity video. The rule-based controller scores 89.6, Claude Haiku 88.7 and optical flow alone 41.9.
  • 15human clicks for the ten tracks with Opus: ten to start them and five on frames it deferred. The annotator using RIPPLE made 158 and scored 97.7.
  • 91.9mean over five repeated runs on that video, with a standard deviation of 0.1. A run costs $1.91, about 1.5 cents per decision.
  • 2 of 5videos on which the loop works well enough to compare annotators. The other three fail for reasons given under Results.

Update, 13 November 2026. A second experiment asks whether the model's tracks can train a tracker. CoTracker3, fine-tuned on tracks that Claude Opus made on cell videos with no human clicks, rose from 42.2 to 49.4 on held-out videos. The numbers are under Cell tracking, and the tracks are in the cell-tracking viewer. The sections above it now use manual tracks as the reference; an earlier version of this page scored against the annotator's RIPPLE tracks.

On two videos, Opus needs about a tenth of the annotator's clicks and gives up about five points. On Neural Activity it made the ten tracks with 15 human clicks against the annotator's 158, at 92.0 against 97.7. On Pinned Down, a jellyfish video, it needed 8 clicks against 116, at 92.1 against 96.9. The rule-based controller used more clicks and scored lower on both.

Repeated runs agree. We ran Opus five times on each of the two videos. APP was 91.9 ± 0.1 on Neural Activity (range 91.7 to 92.0) and 92.1 ± 0.2 on Pinned Down (91.8 to 92.4); human clicks ranged from 12 to 15 and from 6 to 8. In no run did Opus place a position more than 4 px from the manual track. Each decision is one independent API call, so the model has no memory of its earlier decisions and can open no file. A run cost $1.91 and $1.37, about 1.5 cents per decision.

The smaller model is weaker. Claude Haiku scored 3 and 7 points below Opus, and on Neural Activity it fell just below the rule-based controller. It placed 3 and 5 positions more than 4 px from the manual track, where Opus placed none. On Neural Activity the two models gave the same answer on 82 of 91 identical queries. Haiku costs about a thirtieth as much. An earlier version of this page reported that Haiku matched Opus; in those runs each model kept a memory of its decisions along a track, and the score was agreement with the annotator's own RIPPLE track.

Two small open-weight models failed in an earlier test. Qwen3-VL 8B and Qwen2.5-VL 7B, run on a laptop, scored 60.4 and 56.4 on Neural Activity under the earlier protocol, where Opus scored 92.0 and the rule-based controller 88.5. Both answered with about 0.9 confidence and were often wrong, and neither deferred when it should have. They have not been run under the protocol used here.

On the 256 px grid that the RIPPLE paper uses, every method but optical flow scores 99.0 or more, except Opus at 94.9: on one track it moved to a neighbouring cell body, did not defer, and stayed a median 105 px from the manual track.
  • Freely: the loop fails before the model is asked. The targets move 6 to 8 px per frame and optical flow loses them. Even a person at every flagged frame reaches only 53.7.
  • Sperm: not a usable test as it stands. The annotator's own RIPPLE track scores 25.2 against the manual tracks. Several targets are points along a tail with no spot to detect, 553 of the 2,400 frames have no manual position, and three of the six tracks ran out of their budget of 60 decisions.

How it works

the correction loop

  1. Start. Each track starts at the manual position in its first frame, which counts as one human click. RIPPLE's own propagation code builds the track from optical flow.
  2. Flag. A frame is reviewed when forward and backward motion estimates disagree, when the patch under the track stops resembling the target, or when 60 frames have passed without a check.
  3. Propose. For the flagged frame the system lists up to six candidate positions: the tracker's current position, blob detections nearby, and the positions predicted from image motion.
  4. Decide. The language model sees a trusted reference crop, the frames in between, and the flagged frame with the candidates marked. It picks one candidate or abstains.
  5. Defer. An abstention, or a pick the model rates below 0.6 confidence, goes to the simulated person and counts as one human click. Each new position becomes an anchor and RIPPLE rebuilds the track.

One decision: track 1, frame 71

Claude Opus chose B, confidence 0.85. “In frame 71 that terminal blob sits at B, which matches the reference appearance strongly (0.95) and lies 0.6 px from the forward prediction. A falls just off the blob, slightly below and to the left of it.”

A is the tracker's position before the correction. Each crop is 65 px wide, with contrast stretched to its centre so that a faint target stays visible beside a bright neighbour.

What these numbers are not

read before quoting

  • A perfect simulated person. Every track starts at the exact manual position and every deferred frame is answered with it. A real person clicks a pixel or two off and takes time.
  • Two videos carry the result. Thirteen tracks, on Neural Activity and Pinned Down. On the other three videos the loop fails or the test cannot tell the methods apart.
  • Settings tuned on the test tracks. We chose the flagging thresholds and the 60-frame review interval on the ten Neural Activity tracks, against the annotator's RIPPLE tracks. On Pinned Down we changed one setting after looking at the frames, so that candidates are dark spots. The other three videos run on first guesses. We did not tune the rule-based controller's weights, so it is a weak version of that baseline.
  • Variation is over model calls only. The five repeats show how much Opus varies from run to run on the same tracks, not across tracks, videos or annotators. Haiku, and both models on the other three videos, had one run each.
  • One model family under this protocol. Only Claude Opus and Claude Haiku. The open-weight models were tested under the earlier protocol, and larger ones are untested.
  • Pixels, not the paper's grid. APP here is in each video's own pixels. The RIPPLE paper rescales every video to 256 px first; scored that way, Opus gets 98.7 on Neural Activity and 71.8 on Pinned Down.
  • One manual track per target. We have no second manual annotation to show how far two people differ.

Cell tracking

new · training a tracker on the model's tracks

Can the model's tracks train a tracker? CoTracker3, a general point tracker, follows points well in everyday video and poorly in cell microscopy: it scores 76.9 on the TAP-Vid-DAVIS benchmark and 42.2 on the cell videos used here. Fine-tuning it takes tracks from the new kind of video, and those are costly to annotate. Here Claude Opus made the tracks with no human clicks. It marked the cells in the first frame of three unlabelled videos and then said, every 10 frames, where each cell was; CoTracker3 filled in the frames between its marks. When a cell divided, the model marked the two daughters and followed them too. We fine-tuned CoTracker3 on these tracks and tested it on three other videos.

  • 49.4position accuracy on held-out videos after fine-tuning on the model's tracks. The tracker as released scores 42.2.
  • 0human clicks. The model followed 78 cells over 2,592 frames and answered 335 questions.
  • 42.9when the tracker trains instead on its own tracks from the same starting marks.
  • 55.7with the reference tracks of all 249 cells. The model's tracks close about half of that gap.

Position accuracy is the percentage of scored positions within 1, 2, 4, 8 and 16 px of the reference track, averaged over the five distances, with each video scaled to 256 × 256 (the TAP-Vid measure). Every tracker starts each test cell at its first reference position. Each fine-tuning run took 1,000 steps. Brackets give the score of each seed; the other columns are means over seeds.

The model's tracks help; the tracker's own do not. Trained on its own tracks from the same 78 starting marks, CoTracker3 scored 42.9, with seeds on both sides of the untrained 42.2. Trained on the model's tracks it scored 49.4, and the three seeds lie within 1.6 points of each other.

Following daughter cells added 2.6 points. With only the 36 cells that the model followed from the first frame, the score was 46.8. Many of those tracks are short: in the fluorescence video, 14 of the 20 are 11 frames or shorter. The 42 daughters nearly doubled the labelled frames, from 1,324 to 2,592.

The gain is uneven across cell types. On the phase-contrast video the score rose from 37.6 to 52.1, above the 44.5 of the tracker trained on all reference tracks. On the DIC video it rose from 31.7 to 37.1. On the fluorescence video it barely moved, from 57.1 to 58.9, while reference tracks reach 78.3. We cannot yet explain the fluorescence result: the model's tracks there lie a median 2.2 px from the reference.

How the tracks were made

  1. Mark. The model views the first frame in tiles and marks every cell it finds. All 9 cells found in the DIC video and all 7 in the phase-contrast video were followed, and 20 of the 39 found in the fluorescence video, drawn at random.
  2. Follow. Every 10 frames the model views zoomed crops of the last frame where it placed the cell, of a frame in between, and of the new frame. It gives the cell's position, or says that the cell has divided, has ended, or that it is unsure.
  3. Stop. An unsure answer, or a position rated below 0.6 confidence, is skipped. Two skips in a row end the track.
  4. Divide. When a cell divides, the model marks its two daughters and each is followed as a new cell. Daughters born in the last 15 frames, and daughters of daughters, were not followed.
  5. Fill in. CoTracker3 as released tracks from every mark, and the tracks from one cell's marks are blended into a single track that passes through each mark.

Read before quoting

  • Labelled once. We repeated the fine-tuning with three seeds, but the model labelled the videos once. We have not measured how much its labels vary from run to run.
  • Few videos, few cells. One training video and one test video per cell type. The test videos hold 572, 24 and 9 cells, and the score averages the three videos equally, so nine phase-contrast cells carry a third of it.
  • Test cells start from the reference. Every tracker starts each test cell at its first reference position, as point-tracking benchmarks do. Finding the cells is not tested.
  • No cell-tracking baseline yet. We have not compared with methods built for cells, which detect every cell and link the detections.
  • One model, run as agents. Only Claude Opus has labelled these videos. It ran as agents inside Claude Code: we recorded no cost per question, and the agents were told to open only each question's images, with nothing to prevent them from opening the reference tracks.
