Faster neuron tracing

LICONN (Tavakoli et al., Nature 2025) maps brain tissue with expansion light microscopy and traces every neuron with a flood-filling network (FFN). Tracing is the expensive step: one network evaluation costs 81.6 GFLOP, and a single dendrite can take thousands of them. We are looking for a faster method that finds the same neurons on any hardware, not just more GPUs.

  • 19.7 msper tracing step: the authors' model in float32 on a V100 (426 ms on a laptop CPU)
  • 10speed-up ideas tested end to end against the authors' proofread segmentation
  • 3.2×per step: float16 with a float32 output head, plus the fast tracer (5.96 vs 19.27 ms on a V100). Passes the owner's 2% rule on 300 fresh seeds; the rule was relaxed from 1% after that result
  • 1.12×in float32 with output bitwise identical: the fast tracer (624 traces checked, in both number formats)

How FFN tracing works

and why it is slow

  1. Seed. Start from one point inside a neuron.
  2. Look. The network sees a 33×33×33-voxel window of the image plus its current guess of the object, and refines the guess.
  3. Move. Wherever the guess reaches the window's edge (probability ≥ 0.6), queue a new window 8 voxels further along.
  4. Repeat until nothing is left to explore. One object takes hundreds to thousands of steps, one after another.

Cost = number of steps × cost per step. That gives two levers:

A take fewer steps (skip or postpone moves that add little);
B make each step cheaper (smaller network, lower precision, less overhead).

Each step depends on the ones before it, so the trace is a closed loop. A tiny change early can steer it somewhere else later. That turned out to be the central finding.

How results are judged

fixed before any idea was tried

  • Reference: the authors' released model and settings (google/ffn), reproduced bitwise on CPU and confirmed on GPU.
  • Ground truth: the authors' proofread segmentation of the same tissue (500³-voxel crop, 18×18×24 nm voxels).
  • Benchmark: 12 seeds (axons and dendrites) chosen by a fixed rule.
  • Held-out check: a second crop with 12 new seeds, used only to confirm a winner.
  • 300-seed set: 13 further crops chosen by a fixed rule, 300 fresh seeds.
  • Fresh confirmation set: 28 more crops in the same order, 300 new seeds, never used for any choice.

Tolerance (owner-set)

Over 300 seeds, against the reference, a faster variant passes only if:

  • at most 6 seeds (2%) gain a new merge (precision falls by more than 10 points);
  • at most 6 seeds (2%) stop early (recall falls by more than 10 points);
  • mean recall and mean precision each fall by at most 1 point.

How the rule changed: every seed within 1% → at most 3 of 300 (1%), after tiny numerical changes turned out to flip rare merges either way → 2%, set by the owner after the fresh-seed result (below). A pass under 2% is therefore a pass under a rule relaxed after seeing the data. Every number on this page comes from runs whose code and outputs are hash-pinned and re-checked by tests.

Finding the fix

300 seeds, then a diagnosis

On the 300-seed rule, float16 failed: not on merges (3, at the limit) but because 10 traces stopped early. To see why, every move decision of 20 traces was logged in both precisions and replayed on identical inputs.

  1. Where moves are decided. FFN moves where a face of its box is confident. 91% of those faces sit on a saturated plateau, with scores around 2.94, far above the 0.6 threshold.
  2. What float16 does there. Rounding to about 3 digits likely makes several plateau voxels tie, so the move goes to a neighbouring voxel. The trace drifts, and some drifted traces end sooner. This is a hypothesis; the two hypotheses fixed in advance (moves falling just below 0.6, a bias against moving) were not supported.
  3. The fix. Keep the network in float16 but compute only its tiny output head in float32. Changed move decisions fell from 84% to 16% of steps, at the same speed.

Where a step's time goes, and the fast tracer

same output, less overhead

Profiling one float16 step (10.2 ms on a V100) showed that only about 40% was the network itself. The rest was bookkeeping around it: assembling the input, updating the canvas, handing work between threads, and copying.

The fast tracer removes that overhead without changing a single number of FFN's output:

  • The image and the canvas stay on the GPU.
  • The network is called directly, with no executor threads.
  • Input assembly, the canvas update and the six face maxima that decide the next moves run as small compiled GPU functions.
  • Only six scores and six positions come back to the CPU, where FFN's own queue code runs unchanged.

Both paths' timers exclude per-trace setup, about 0.6 s per trace, mostly copying the image to the GPU. Counting it, the float32-head gain is 1.37× rather than 1.43× (exploratory measurement).

  • Accuracy carries over. Every trace is bitwise identical to the network it speeds up, so the 2% verdict for the float32-head network stands unchanged.
  • Exactness needed care. FFN picks moves with NumPy, where the first NaN counts as the maximum. The GPU's argmax does not reliably return the first of several NaNs, so traces changed (3 of 12 benchmark seeds and 4 of the first 168 fresh seeds, with the float32-head network) until NaNs were located explicitly.
  • Doing it naively was slower. Computing the six face maxima as six separate GPU reductions took 6.83 ms per step, slower than leaving them on the CPU (6.36 ms). One stacked reduction brought it to 5.96 ms.
  • What is left. The network is now about 4.6 of the 5.96 ms. Going much further needs a faster network, or a different method (below).

A different approach: a single-pass ViT

in progress: training started October 24

FFN finds one neuron at a time, one small window after another. Even with the fast tracer, a neuron takes about 2.5 s and hundreds of steps. The owner asked for a different kind of model: a vision transformer (ViT) that looks at a whole 128³-voxel block once and predicts, for every voxel, whether it belongs with its neighbours. That segments all neurons in the block in one pass. A timing probe of a simpler version took 42 ms per block on a V100. No accuracy result exists yet.

Architecture

On a phone, scroll the diagram sideways.

  • Type: a hybrid of a vision transformer and a U-Net (in the style of UNETR and TransUNet), written in JAX/Flax.
  • The single pass: 8 transformer blocks with global self-attention (6 heads, MLP width 1,536) over 4,096 tokens. Each token covers 8×8×8 voxels (144×144×192 nm), and every token attends to the whole block.
  • Why the convolutions: a token is wider than a thin axon. The convolutional encoder and its skip connections carry full- and half-resolution detail to the decoder. An independent model check pointed out that without them, thin axons could not be resolved.
  • Size: 16.8 M parameters, 14.2 M of them in the transformer. FFN's network has 20 residual blocks and works on a 33³ window.
  • Numbers: float16 arithmetic with float32 weights.

What it predicts: affinities

For every voxel v and six offsets o, the model predicts how likely v and v−o belong to the same neuron.

  • 3 nearest neighbours: one voxel back in z, y and x. These build the segmentation.
  • 3 long-range: 3 voxels in z, 4 in y and x, each 72 nm. They are extra training targets that make the model use context.

Targets come from the proofread labels. Both voxels in the same labelled neuron: 1. Different labels, at least one labelled: 0, a boundary. Both unlabelled: ignored, because about half of each crop has no label.

How the loss is calculated

L = ⅙ Σc Σv wc,v · BCE(zc,v, yc,v)

wc,v = mc,v · ( yc,v / 2Pc + (1−yc,v) / 2Nc )

z: predicted logit; y: target (0 or 1); m: 1 unless the pair is ignored; Pc, Nc: numbers of positive and negative targets of channel c in the batch; BCE: binary cross-entropy on the sigmoid.

  • Class-balanced. Inside neurons, “same neuron” pairs outnumber boundary pairs by tens to one. Each channel's positives and negatives therefore get equal total weight, so the model cannot do well by ignoring boundaries.
  • Averaged over the 6 channels, then over the batch. A worked example below shows every number on a small patch.
  • Stable in float16: the loss is scaled by 1,024 before gradients are taken. Any step with a non-finite gradient is skipped and counted; more than 1% skipped marks the run as unstable.

Training

DataProofread crops 0–11 (500³ voxels each) for training. Crop 12 for choosing the checkpoint and the merge threshold. The 28 fresh crops with the 300 fresh seeds are the test set; the benchmark and held-out crops are never used.
Batches2 random 128³ blocks per step. Flips in z, y and x, swapping y and x (z is never swapped, as its voxels are larger), and small changes of brightness and contrast.
OptimiserAdamW, peak learning rate 3·10−4 after 1,000 warm-up steps, cosine decay to zero, weight decay 0.05.
Length170,000 steps, fixed in advance from a timing run (0.74 s per step on one V100): about 35 hours.
CheckpointEvery 2,000 steps the loss is measured on 8 fixed blocks of crop 12. The lowest-loss checkpoint is the model that is tested, which guards against overfitting 12 crops.

From affinities to neurons

  1. Tile. 128³ blocks every 96 voxels cover a crop; each voxel is taken from exactly one block.
  2. Boundary map. One minus the mean affinity of the six edges around each voxel.
  3. Fragments. A watershed grows small fragments from the centres of confident regions, so even a thin axon gets its own fragment.
  4. Merge. Repeatedly join the two touching fragments with the highest mean affinity across their contact, counting only confident voxels, until the best is below a threshold. The threshold is chosen on crop 12.
  5. Score. For each seed, take the segment that contains it and score it exactly as FFN's traces are scored.

On a phone, scroll the figure sideways.

What the model predicts: edges, not voxels. The input is a 128³-voxel block of image intensities (A, B). The output is 6 numbers for every voxel, a 6×128³ array. Each number belongs to an edge, a pair of voxels: this voxel and the one before it in z, in y and in x, then 3 slices back, 4 voxels up and 4 voxels left. It is the predicted probability that the two voxels belong to the same neuron (C). Every edge is predicted once. The edge to a voxel's right-hand neighbour is that neighbour's “x−1” number.

Everything else is computed from these edge numbers, not predicted. The voxel score (panels 2 and 4) is the average of the 6 nearest edges around one voxel; one minus it is the boundary map. It decides which voxels are confident and where fragments form. The contact score (panel 6) is the average of the edges between two fragments. It decides whether they merge. The loss (panel 3) compares each edge number with its target from the labels (panel 1).

Only the in-plane edges to the nearest neighbours are drawn. Each voxel also has an edge to the slice above and one to the slice below, and the voxel scores include them: 6 edges in all. The edges in panel 1 that join the patch to voxels just outside it are not drawn either. The three long-range edges are trained by the loss but are not used in either average.

  • A–C Input and output. The model reads image intensities only. For the red voxel in C it outputs 6 numbers. Here the voxel above and 4 up (same bright neuron) get about 1, and the voxel to the left and 4 left (across the dark gap) get about 0. The slices before it are not drawn.
  • 1 Targets. Each pair of neighbouring voxels is an edge. Its target is 1 if both voxels belong to the same proofread neuron, 0 if their labels differ and at least one is labelled, and ignored if both are unlabelled.
  • 2 Voxel score from the targets. For each voxel, the mean of the targets on its 6 edges, leaving out ignored ones. Deep inside a neuron every edge is 1, so the score is 1. In the right neuron's first column one of the six edges faces the gap, giving 5/6 = .83. On the edge of the gap every labelled edge is a boundary, giving 0. This is the score a perfect model would produce.
  • 3 Predictions and loss. All the model's edge numbers in this patch, drawn over the image they came from. Each probability p is compared with its target from panel 1 by binary cross-entropy, −[y log p + (1−y) log(1−p)], weighted, and summed (table). The loss only ever sees edges, never the averages.
  • 4 Voxel score from the predictions. The same average as panel 2, taken over panel 3's predictions. Every edge counts here, including those in unlabelled space. A voxel is confident (light) when its score is above 0.5, meaning the boundary map is below 0.5. Inside neurons it is close to panel 2.
  • 5 Fragments. Markers are placed at the centres of the confident regions of panel 4: the points farthest from any dark voxel, found in 3D. One falls in this patch (red, in 1690); the other fragments' markers lie in other slices. A watershed grows a fragment from each marker until fragments meet. Every voxel gets a fragment, dark ones included. The result is deliberately over-split.
  • 6 Contact score. For two touching fragments, take the edges that cross from one to the other. Keep those whose two voxels are both light in panel 4, and average their predictions from panel 3. Edges with an unconfident voxel are left out because they sit on boundaries and would drag the mean down even between two pieces of one neuron.

Why the weights differ. The patch's vertical edges have 58 positive and 3 negative targets. Positives share half of the weight and negatives the other half, so each boundary edge weighs 0.5/3 = 0.167 and each interior edge 0.5/58 = 0.0086: about 19× more.

Loss of this patch = mean over its two channels of Σ weight × BCE = (0.027 + 0.084) / 2 = 0.055. Training uses the same formula over all 6 channels of a batch of two 128³ blocks.

How the loss connects to the averages

The loss trains each edge separately. It pulls every prediction in panel 3 towards its target in panel 1. As the edges get closer, the voxel scores in panel 4 get closer to the perfect ones in panel 2, so the confident regions and fragments follow the real neurons. The contact scores in panel 6 move towards 1 between pieces of one neuron and towards 0 between different neurons.

The class balancing matters for these averages. Most edges are interior, so without it a model could predict about 1 everywhere and still score well. Panel 4 would then be light everywhere: the boundaries would vanish, fragments would not separate, and contacts between different neurons would score near 1.

  • M: 1429 + 1690 merge. Both are pieces of the right neuron. In this patch 14 of their 16 crossing edges have two light voxels, with mean prediction 0.96. Over their whole 3D contact, 116 counted edges average 0.93. That is above the 0.85 threshold of the slider example, so they merge.
  • K: 1429 + 1780 stay apart. 1780 is the unlabelled gap. All 5 crossing edges here touch a dark voxel, so none counts. Across the whole 3D contact only 3 edges count, averaging 0.59, below 0.85, so they stay separate.
  • Merging takes the highest contact score first. After each merge, the contacts of the merged fragment are re-averaged over all their counted edges, and it continues while the best score is above the threshold.

How it will be judged

A different method cannot be held to “the same traces as FFN”. The owner set a rule against the proofread ground truth before training. On the 300 fresh seeds, compared with the float32-head FFN:

  • Competitive: mean recall and precision each within 2 points of FFN; net new merges and net new early stops at most 6 each (2%).
  • Promising (worth a larger run): within 5 points; at most 15 each (5%).
  • Otherwise fail.

“Net”: a merge the ViT adds counts against it, and a merge FFN makes that the ViT avoids counts for it. Gross counts and the comparison with float32 FFN are reported alongside.

  • Speed headline, fixed in advance: the ViT's total time for all 28 test crops, GPU and CPU, against FFN's estimated time to trace every neuron of the same size in those crops.
  • Order: crop 12 is run and inspected first. The test crops are segmented once, afterwards.
  • Leakage, disclosed: the crops touch, so a neuron can cross from a training crop into a test crop; those seeds are reported separately. FFN's own network was trained on this volume too.
  • Checked by two other models before training. Gemini found five design flaws, all fixed. They included a boundary map that looked only one way, so one face of every neurite looked like a boundary. Grok asked for crop 12 to be inspected first and the speed headline to be fixed in advance.

The workflow, for every milestone

On a phone, scroll the chart sideways.

How to read it

  1. The owner sets the goal and the rules. What to achieve, the accuracy tolerance, which data and how much compute.
  2. Claude Code proposes the next milestone and drafts its contract: the pre-registered plan for that step (real example below), filled in from a fixed template.
  3. The owner approves the plan, explained in plain words, or changes it. The owner never writes the contract; they decide what it commits to.
  4. The controller freezes it. The controller is a plain script, not an AI. It records the contract, fingerprints the code and runs the unmodified code as a baseline.
  5. Claude Code does the engineering: code, cluster jobs, analysis.
  6. A second model checks the method from the contract and the original sources only, so it cannot inherit the implementer's assumptions.
  7. The controller re-executes everything in a throw-away copy and hashes the code and outputs. An agent saying "it works" is not evidence.
  8. A third model reviews the change against that evidence. If it fails: at most two repairs, then back to the owner.
  9. The owner makes the call. Adopt, move on, or change the rules. A rule change is logged and applies only going forward.

Why it is reliable

  • Separation of roles: the model that writes the code never grades it.
  • No moving goalposts: the tolerance and tests are frozen, and only the owner can change them.
  • Bounded retries: no "retry until it passes"; failures are kept as results.
  • Traceable numbers: every figure links to hashed code and outputs, and any later edit marks it stale.
  • Human judgement where it matters: on research meaning, not on boilerplate.
