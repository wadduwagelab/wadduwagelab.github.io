Faster neuron tracing

LICONN (Tavakoli et al., Nature 2025) maps brain tissue with expansion light microscopy and traces every neuron with a flood-filling network (FFN). Tracing is the expensive step: one network evaluation costs 81.6 GFLOP, and a single dendrite can take thousands of them. We are looking for a faster method that finds the same neurons on any hardware, not just more GPUs.

  • 19.7 msper tracing step: the authors' model in float32 on a V100 (426 ms on a laptop CPU)

How FFN tracing works

and why it is slow

  1. Seed. Start from one point inside a neuron.
  2. Look. The network sees a 33×33×33-voxel window of the image plus its current guess of the object, and refines the guess.
  3. Move. Wherever the guess reaches the window's edge (probability ≥ 0.6), queue a new window 8 voxels further along.
  4. Repeat until nothing is left to explore. One object takes hundreds to thousands of steps, one after another.

Cost = number of steps × cost per step. That gives two levers:

A take fewer steps (skip or postpone moves that add little);
B make each step cheaper (smaller network, lower precision, less overhead).

Each step depends on the ones before it, so the trace is a closed loop. A tiny change early can steer it somewhere else later. That turned out to be the central finding.

How results are judged

fixed before any idea was tried

  • Reference: the authors' released model and settings (google/ffn), reproduced bitwise on CPU and confirmed on GPU.
  • Ground truth: the authors' proofread segmentation of the same tissue (500³-voxel crop, 18×18×24 nm voxels).
  • Benchmark: 12 seeds (axons and dendrites) chosen by a fixed rule.
  • Held-out check: a second crop with 12 new seeds, used only to confirm a winner.

Tolerance

A faster variant passes only if, against the reference:

  • mean recall, precision and unlabeled fraction change by ≤ 1 point;
  • no single seed loses more than 1 recall point;
  • merged voxels grow by ≤ 1%, and there are no new merges with other neurons.

Every number on this page comes from runs whose code and outputs are hash-pinned and re-checked by tests.

Key finding: traces are knife-edge at merge points

float16 changes only 0.006% of the network's per-voxel decisions. On the benchmark that was harmless. On held-out data it pushed two dendrite traces across a thin boundary into a large neighbouring neuron, which the trace then followed for thousands of extra steps.

Where a step's time goes

one float16 step on a V100, 10.2 ms

Only about 40% of the time is the network itself; the rest is bookkeeping around it.

Removing that overhead keeps the arithmetic, and therefore the output, bitwise identical. Calling the network directly already gives 9% faster, identical results in float32 (17.9 vs 19.7 ms). In float32 the network itself takes about 14.7 of the 19.7 ms, so removing all of the overhead around an unchanged float32 network gives at most about 1.3×.

Next

in order

  1. Change the yardstick

    The per-seed 1% rule cannot tell a real loss from a rare merge flip, and those flips occur in both directions. Next, variants will be judged over many more seeds, with an explicit allowance for rare merge flips plus limits on aggregate accuracy. The exact definition is still to be agreed.

  2. Free speed first

    Fuse the bookkeeping (input assembly, canvas update, threading, copies) into the network call. The output stays identical by construction; the gain is up to about 1.3×.

  3. Then a cheaper network

    Re-check float16 under the new yardstick, then try distilling a smaller student network from the authors' model. Distillation needs no new labels, so the public data is enough. Int8 is an untested idea.

How the research is run

faster, without lowering the bar

Everything above took four days (October 18–21, 2026): reading the paper, reproducing the authors' result, setting up a GPU cluster, and testing and ruling out eight ideas, including a held-out check. AI agents did the engineering. A structured workflow kept the science honest. Below are numbers and real examples from this project's own logs.

  • 19task contracts: goal, allowed files, tests and limits frozen before work starts
  • 17independent method checks by a second AI model
  • 28evidence reviews by a third model, on outputs run in a disposable copy
  • 38problems caught and fixed before results were reported (4 high severity)
  • 103automated tests, many re-checking hashes of the code and outputs behind each number
  • 8research decisions made by the human owner, written down before results were seen

Then: the precision screen was re-run at batch 1. The first runs are kept, labelled “superseded”, not deleted.

“No — it works on this machine by environment coincidence (no GPU/Metal plugin installed), not because the config update is placed early enough.”

Then: the CPU-only reference was enforced in code, so it reproduces bitwise on any machine, not just this one.

Then: it was flagged as a scope breach and taken to the owner, who reviewed it and approved that read explicitly. The approval is recorded in the write-up.

“The claim that no residual block can be dropped within tolerance is unsupported. Only block 12 and the combined drop of blocks 12 and 17 received closed-loop benchmarks.”

Then: the conclusion was narrowed to what was actually tested.

OwnerRunnerdesign, not luck

float16 passed all 12 benchmark seeds at 2.19× per step.

Then: the held-out check, fixed in advance, ran 12 new seeds on a separate crop. Two large false merges appeared, so the 2.2× is not claimed. On the benchmark alone it would have looked like a clean win.
